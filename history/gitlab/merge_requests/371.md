Closes #338 (design §5.16). The second and last prerequisite for §6 step 9.

## The rule

§1.3 stated first-hit-wins with **no version qualifier**, and the two caches `_fetch_one` consults had opposite `latest` policies — `Session` maintained a `_latest` index to serve it, the local store explicitly refused to, and `Session` is consulted first so it always won.

The decision splits a question that had been one:

- **A pinned URN names an immutable artefact** and caches everywhere, forever. **HTTP headers get no say.** RFC 9111 constrains caches interposed between a client and an origin, not an application holding an artefact under an identity the standard forbids reusing.
- **`latest` names a mutable pointer** and is never a cache key. What comes back is interned under the pinned URN it resolved to — no extra round-trip, since the payload carries its own version.
- **The pointer's freshness window is the provider's call, with a caller override.** The one place `Cache-Control` legitimately applies. **Not built here — #339.** Until then the pointer is re-asked every time, the safe end of the trade.
- **A provider directive does not veto an explicit `materialise_to`.** If `no-store` did, no ETL pipeline could exist.

Two of the four options originally on the table — "honour HTTP headers" and "resolve `latest` to pinned" — turned out to **compose rather than compete**, which is why the first framing of the decision was wrong.

## Measured from our own cassettes

ECB/IMF are 94 of 173 recorded structure responses, and the same responses stamp `isFinal="false"` on all 2420 artefacts — appliance defaults, not assertions about volatility. That is why obedience alone is the wrong default and the override is part of the design.

## Why it is small

The resolve-to-pinned half already worked. `_latest` / `_resolved_latest` lost their only readers and are deleted, and with them `_version_key` — a second, cruder version parser that existed only to pick their winner. Its two Hypothesis properties now run against `model/version.py`'s `sort_key`, the one the storage layer bakes.

## Two integration tests broke, and both were findings

**`test_registry_cache_hit`** asserted a cache hit that was really the staleness bug. Re-pointed at a pinned URN, where the hit is legitimate.

**`test_resolve_partial_false_has_more_codes_than_partial` had never exercised its claim.** Its cassette holds a *single* `detail=referencepartial` interaction — the `partial=False` call was answered from `_resolved_latest`, so `detail=full` was never issued even at record time, and `full >= partial` compared an object with itself.

That is worse than staleness: a caller writing `partial=False` is saying *"I need the untrimmed codelists"* and was silently handed the trimmed ones. **Skipped pending a re-recorded cassette** (needs network against ECB — flagging rather than fabricating recorded provider data); `tests/unit/test_rest_partial.py` guards the behaviour meanwhile and fails **4/4** against the old code.

## Evidence

- `tests/unit/test_rest_latest.py`: 4 of 6 fail against the original `session.py`; the other 2 fail when the pinned cache is disabled — so both halves of the rule discriminate. Every test publishes a new version *between two calls*, the only arrangement that tells a fresh answer from a lucky one.
- A test-design mistake while writing these surfaced behaviour worth recording: a later, fuller fetch **upgrades the interned artefact in place**, so a caller holding the earlier DSD sees its codelist grow. Not a stale-cache bug — the same pinned artefact, more completely known — but it means "read, re-fetch, compare" measures nothing. Now its own test.
- Suppressions dropped 20 → 18 with the deleted code.
